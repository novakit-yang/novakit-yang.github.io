Sidechain Ducking in REAPER: Music That Gets Out of the Way

By NovaKit · 6 min read · REAPER 7

A music bed under speech has one job: be there when nobody is talking, and get out of the way when somebody is. Doing that by hand means drawing automation across forty minutes of conversation. Doing it with a sidechain means wiring one send once.

Why volume automation stops scaling

On a two-minute promo, drawing the music down by hand is fine. On a four-person, forty-minute episode it is not, for three reasons:

  • Overlaps. People talk over each other. The music has to come down before the first syllable and stay down through the crosstalk, which means judging each boundary by ear, dozens of times.
  • Edits. Every cut you make afterwards shifts every automation point. Re-time the intro and you get to redraw the first two minutes.
  • Consistency. Hand-drawn ducks are never the same depth twice, and the ear notices a bed that breathes unevenly far more than one that is slightly too low.

A sidechain does the same job from the dialogue itself: the music is pulled down by the voices, at the speed the voices actually arrive.

The routing, in five steps

The session needs a DIALOGUE BUS that all the voices feed, and a MUSIC BUS that carries the bed. Both are folder tracks.

  1. Open the I/O panel on DIALOGUE BUS and add a send to MUSIC BUS.
  2. Set the send's source to Audio 1/2 and its destination to Audio 3/4. Not 1/2 — see below.
  3. Insert ReaComp on MUSIC BUS.
  4. In ReaComp, set Detector input to Auxiliary input L+R. This is the whole trick: the compressor now listens to what arrives on channels 3/4 instead of its own input.
  5. Turn the music down with the compressor's threshold until the gain-reduction meter moves when somebody speaks, and returns to zero when they stop.
Why channels 3/4. If you send the dialogue to the music bus on 1/2, the music bus passes it on to the parent and the voices are heard twice: once clean through the dialogue bus, once through the music bus, slightly delayed by whatever latency the chain adds. Auxiliary channels 3/4 exist precisely so a track can receive audio as a control signal rather than as something to play.

Setting the four controls

These are starting points, not settings to copy blindly — every voice and every bed is different.

ControlStarting pointWhat it does to the mix
ThresholdLow enough that normal speech triggers it, high enough that room tone does notSets the point where ducking begins. Too high and quiet guests never trigger
RatioModerate — around 4:1 to 6:1Sets how far the music drops. Extreme ratios make the bed disappear
AttackFast, but not instant — roughly 10–20 msToo slow and the first syllable collides with the music; too fast and the duck sounds like a click
ReleaseLong — long enough to span the gap between sentencesThe single most important control. Too short and the bed pumps up and down between every phrase

Two more that are easy to forget: a small pre-comp lets the compressor start ducking a few milliseconds before the voice arrives, which hides the attack entirely; and makeup gain restores the level the duck removed, so the music sits at the right height when nobody is speaking.

Full-band, not multiband — and why

Ducking only the midrange looks like the elegant answer: pull down 500 Hz–3 kHz and leave the bass and cymbals alone. It does not work in REAPER, and the reason is specific:

ReaXcomp has no external sidechain detector input. REAPER's multiband compressor can only listen to its own input, so band-limited ducking driven by another track is not possible without extra routing. A full-band duck with a well-set release is what most speech-over-music mixes want anyway: the bed is already EQ'd to sit under the voice, so pulling the whole thing down sounds natural rather than surgical.

Verifying it by ear, not by eye

  • Watch the gain-reduction meter while a full sentence plays: it should move, and it should return to zero in the pauses.
  • Solo the music bus and listen to a whole paragraph — if you can hear the bed stepping up and down, the release is too short.
  • Then unsolo and listen again: the duck should be invisible. If you notice it, it is too deep.
  • Check the end of the episode: when the music returns after the last word, it should rise over a second or so, not snap back.

When to still use automation

Sidechain ducking is for the running bed. Two places still want hand-drawn fades: the intro (music up, voice enters) and the outro (voice out, music resolves). A sidechain will technically handle both, but neither wants to be triggered by speech — they want a shape you decided on. Use a volume envelope for those two moments and let the sidechain handle everything between them.

When it does not work, it is almost always one of five things

SymptomCause
Hear the voices twice, slightly hollowThe send was set to audio 1/2 instead of 3/4, so the music bus is playing the dialogue as well as receiving it
Gain reduction meter never movesDetector input left on the main input — ReaComp is listening to the music, which never stops
Ducks, but always a fraction lateAttack too slow, or no pre-comp. The music starts dropping after the first consonant
Bed pumps up and down between phrasesRelease too short. This is the most common mistake by a wide margin
Music disappears whenever anyone speaksRatio and threshold both set too aggressively. Ducking should be felt, not heard

There is also a routing trap worth naming: the send has to come from the bus, not from the four individual voice tracks. Four sends to the same destination sum four times, so the compressor sees a signal roughly 12 dB hotter than any single voice and ducks hard on room tone alone. One send, from the bus, is the correct shape.

Checklist

  • Send from the dialogue bus to the music bus, source 1/2 → destination 3/4;
  • ReaComp on the music bus, detector set to auxiliary input;
  • Release long enough to span sentence gaps;
  • Makeup gain restored so the bed sits right between lines;
  • Hand-drawn fades for intro and outro only.
