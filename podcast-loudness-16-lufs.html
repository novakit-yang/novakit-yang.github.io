Loudness & Delivery

How to Hit −16 LUFS for a Podcast in REAPER (Stock Plug-Ins Only)

By NovaKit · 7 min read · REAPER 7

Loudness is the one delivery requirement that every podcast platform enforces and almost nobody measures properly. This is how to land on −16 LUFS integrated with a −1.0 dBTP ceiling inside REAPER, using nothing but what ships with the program.

Why −16 LUFS, and why not “as loud as possible”

Every major platform has a target and turns louder material down to meet it:

PlatformNormalisation target
Apple Podcasts−16 LUFS (stereo)
Spotify−14 LUFS
YouTube−14 LUFS
Amazon Music / Audible−14 to −16 LUFS

The consequence is asymmetric, and this is the part people miss: platforms turn loud material down, they do not turn quiet material up. So a mix delivered at −11 LUFS arrives at the listener at −14 anyway, but with all the transient detail crushed out of it by your own limiter. A mix delivered at −16 keeps its dynamics and lands exactly where it was intended.

−16 is therefore the practical choice: it is Apple's target, it is within a rounding error of everyone else's, and it leaves the mix sounding like a recording rather than a commercial.

Three numbers people mix up

MeasureWhat it isWhat it is for
LUFS-I (integrated)Loudness averaged across the whole programmeThe delivery number. This is what −16 refers to
LUFS-S (short-term)Loudness over a rolling ~3 second windowCatching a section that drifts loud or quiet
dBTP (true peak)Peak level, including the inter-sample peaks a normal meter missesAvoiding distortion once the file is encoded to MP3 or AAC

Peak level and loudness are not the same thing and cannot be traded for each other. A file can sit at −1.0 dBTP and still be 8 LU too quiet.

Measure it inside REAPER, no plug-ins needed

Right-click the master track's VU meter and enable the loudness display. You get a live LUFS-M reading and, once you play from the start, an integrated figure that keeps updating. That is enough to answer “is this episode in the right place?” without installing a metering suite.

Two practical notes:

  • Play the whole episode. An integrated reading taken from the first 30 seconds is not an integrated reading. Intros are usually louder than conversation and will give you a misleading number.
  • Do not measure a mono file as dual mono. The same signal in both channels sums to roughly 3 LU higher than a single channel. If a guest sent you a mono recording, check how REAPER is placing it before you trust the number.

Two ways to hit the target, one of them correct

The wrong way: push the limiter until the meter reads −16. This works numerically and ruins the mix. You are using a peak limiter as a gain control, so it flattens every transient in the programme to raise the average.

The right way: separate the two jobs. The limiter's only task is to stop peaks crossing the ceiling. The loudness job happens at render, where REAPER can apply one gain change to the finished file.

Open File → Render and set the normalisation mode to LUFS-I with a target of −16 and a true-peak limit of −1.0 dBTP. REAPER measures the rendered programme and applies the correction automatically. Because the settings are stored inside the .RPP file, they travel with the project instead of living in your preferences — open the same session on another machine and the render is still correct.

Why the ceiling is −1.0 dBTP and not 0.0

MP3 and AAC are lossy formats. The encoding process changes the waveform, and the reconstructed peaks can come out higher than the peaks in the WAV you started from. A file that measures −0.1 dBTP before encoding can clip after it.

−1.0 dBTP is the margin that absorbs that overshoot. It costs you nothing audible and it is the number every major platform publishes as its delivery ceiling.

Order matters. Limit first, normalise second. If you normalise up to the target and then need more limiting, you will be limiting an already-normalised file and undoing the normalisation. The render dialog applies them in the correct order for you: it normalises, then limits to the ceiling.

Where this usually goes wrong

SymptomCause
Sounds loud but flat and tiringLoudness was raised with the limiter instead of at render
Episodes vary in level week to weekTarget measured by ear, or from a short section
Distortion only on the MP3, not the WAVNo true-peak ceiling; lossy encoding pushed peaks over
Reaches −16 on your machine, not on a client'sRender settings saved in preferences rather than in the project

If the recording already arrived too hot

Common in multi-speaker sessions: one guest recorded with their interface gain high and their file peaks near 0 dB, while everyone else sits 10 dB lower. This is recoverable, but the order matters.

Pull that file down first, before it hits its own chain, using the item volume handle — not the track fader. Item volume happens before the chain, so the compressor on that track sees a signal in the right range instead of one that is permanently over threshold. Trim first, then process. Doing it the other way round means the compressor has already squashed the loud guest before you turned anything down, and turning the fader down afterwards only makes a squashed voice quieter.

What is not recoverable is clipping. If the waveform is already flat-topped, the peaks are gone and no amount of gain staging brings them back. That is a re-record, or an honest note in the show notes.

What to check before you upload

Checklist

  • Limiter ceiling at −1.0 dBTP, threshold just under it — the limiter is a fuse, not a compressor;
  • Render normalisation: LUFS-I, −16, true peak −1.0 dBTP;
  • 48 kHz, and both delivery formats in one pass;
  • Settings saved inside the project file, not in preferences;
  • Measure the whole episode, once, before you upload.
